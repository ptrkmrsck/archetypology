# Theoretical Grounding

Reference when writing or editing type portrait prose. These are the phenomenological bedrock that every portrait must honor; Hillman's imaginal lens and Beebe's archetypal model extend them, but do not contradict them.

## Jung's Eight Function-Attitudes

Drawn from Jung's _Psychological Types_ (1921). When writing about a function in any archetypal position, begin from what Jung observed, then let it darken, brighten, or twist according to its position in the stack.

**Extraverted Thinking (Te):** Orients judgment by objective, external criteria: facts, metrics, systems, logical consistency verified against the outer world. Organizes reality into efficient structures. At its best, builds; at its worst, reduces the living to the measurable. Jung noted it subordinates all else to an intellectual formula derived from external data.

**Introverted Thinking (Ti):** Seeks internal logical coherence; builds frameworks from subjective first principles rather than external evidence. Follows the inner idea wherever it leads, even when the world does not confirm it. At its best, achieves depth of conceptual precision; at its worst, becomes detached from reality, spinning systems in a void. Jung described it as oriented by the subjective factor, an inner image whose origin is not external.

**Extraverted Feeling (Fe):** Evaluates by collective values, social harmony, and shared emotional atmosphere. Reads and orchestrates the feeling-tone of a room. Seeks rapport, consensus, the appropriate. At its best, creates warmth and belonging; at its worst, loses the individual in the crowd, performing feeling without possessing it. Jung noted it adjusts itself to objective circumstances and prevailing values.

**Introverted Feeling (Fi):** Evaluates by deeply personal, internal values that are felt rather than articulated. Still waters; intensity runs below the surface. Seeks authenticity, resonance between inner state and outer expression. At its best, possesses a moral compass of extraordinary sensitivity; at its worst, retreats into incommunicable depths. Jung described it as intensive rather than extensive, developing in depth.

**Extraverted Sensation (Se):** Perceives the concrete, immediate, sensory world with vivid acuity. Lives in the present tense of physical reality: color, texture, force, impact. Seeks intensity of experience. At its best, fully alive to the moment; at its worst, addicted to stimulation, unable to see past the surface. Jung called it the sensuous perception of the object.

**Introverted Sensation (Si):** Perceives not the raw object but the subjective impression the object evokes; the inner image overlaid on experience. Preserves, compares, stores. Sees the present through the depth of past impressions. At its best, possesses a richness of inner imagery that stabilizes and deepens experience; at its worst, mistakes the internal image for external reality. Jung described it as an unconscious disposition which alters the sense-perception at its source.

**Extraverted Intuition (Ne):** Perceives possibilities, latent potentials, and connections radiating outward from objects and situations. Scans the horizon for what could be, what is emerging, what is not yet. At its best, visionary, generative, alive to the new; at its worst, scattered, unable to commit, always leaping before the fruit is ripe. Jung described it as the function that "sees round corners."

**Introverted Intuition (Ni):** Perceives the archetypal image arising from the depths; receives visions, symbolic impressions, a sense of where things are going that has little to do with external data. Converges rather than diverges: seeks _the_ meaning, not many meanings. At its best, prophetic, penetrating to the essence; at its worst, possessed by a single inner vision that admits no correction from the world. Jung described its images as having the value of specific insights which are of equal importance to the ideas of the thinking type.

## Jung's Eight Type Portraits

From Chapter X of _Psychological Types_ (1921). These are portraits of _persons_, not functions in isolation. Every MBTI type maps to one of these eight (two per Jungian type, differentiated by the auxiliary). The dominant's portrait is the phenomenological bedrock; the auxiliary determines which direction that dominant energy is channeled.

**The Extraverted Thinking Type (ENTJ, ESTJ):** Life is organized by an intellectual formula oriented to objective data. This type builds systems, implements plans, measures outcomes. Feeling is repressed and, when it surfaces, is primitive: sentiment or resentment, rarely nuance. The danger is tyranny of the formula, reducing all of life to what can be quantified and administered. The shadow emerges as unconscious sentimentality or cold ruthlessness toward anyone who defies the system. Jung observed that the repressed feeling function may erupt in devotion to unworthy causes or attachments that baffle the thinker's own logic.

**The Introverted Thinking Type (INTP, ISTP):** Thought follows internal logic rather than external evidence; the inner idea has more authority than the fact. This type pursues conceptual depth with tenacity, building frameworks that may be brilliant but increasingly disconnected from shared reality. Feeling is inferior and manifests as social awkwardness, suspicion, or fierce attachment to a few. The danger is the ivory tower: ideas that admit no correction from the world. Jung noted a characteristic stubbornness, an inner certainty that holds fast against all external objection, and a relationship to the world that is frequently defensive.

**The Extraverted Feeling Type (ENFJ, ESFJ):** Consciousness is organized by collective feeling-values, the "appropriate," the harmonious, the socially sanctioned. This type reads the emotional atmosphere of a room and adjusts to it with remarkable fluidity. Thinking is repressed and, when it surfaces, is crude: rigid, reductive, or dogmatic. The danger is loss of individual feeling: performing warmth without possessing it, becoming a vessel for the group's emotional needs while the personal soul starves. Jung described a characteristic "extinction of the subject's own personality" in favor of the object.

**The Introverted Feeling Type (INFP, ISFP):** Still waters running deep. Feeling is intense, personal, and largely incommunicable; it does not announce itself but radiates a quiet influence. This type evaluates everything by inner resonance: authentic or inauthentic, aligned or false. Thinking is inferior and appears as harsh generalizations, black-and-white judgments, or paranoid interpretations when the type is under stress. The danger is inaccessibility: so much happens below the surface that others perceive only silence or moodiness. Jung called this type "harmonious but difficult to read," with a tendency to "let herself be violated rather than take defensive action."

**The Extraverted Sensation Type (ESFP, ESTP):** Perception is seized by the concrete, immediate, sensory object with an intensity that admits no abstraction. This type lives in the vivid present: taste, texture, impact, the body's knowledge. Intuition is inferior and manifests as dark premonitions, superstition, or suspicion of hidden meanings. The danger is enslavement to stimulation: compulsive experience-seeking, an inability to say no to the senses. Jung noted that the repressed intuition may project itself as jealousy, foreboding, or attachment to mystical or occult ideas that the conscious personality would dismiss.

**The Introverted Sensation Type (ISFJ, ISTJ):** Perception is shaped by the subjective impression overlaid on the object; not the thing itself but the inner image it evokes. This type sees the present through accumulated past impressions, preserving, comparing, stabilizing. Intuition is inferior and erupts as vague dread, catastrophic imaginings, or obsessive worry about unseen threats. The danger is substituting the internal image for external reality: insisting the world is as it has always appeared to inner experience, even when it has changed. Jung described a characteristic difficulty in communicating the richness of inner perception, which to others may appear as mere pedantry or stubbornness.

**The Extraverted Intuitive Type (ENFP, ENTP):** Perception scans the horizon for possibility, latent potential, what is emerging. This type is drawn to the new, the not-yet, the promising beginning; commitment to any single actuality is the great difficulty. Sensation is inferior and manifests as neglect of the body, ignorance of physical limits, or sudden eruptions of sensory indulgence. The danger is the eternal sowing without harvest: abandoning each project the moment its novelty fades. Jung observed that the repressed sensation may chain the intuitive to relationships, possessions, or physical obsessions that the conscious personality considers beneath it.

**The Introverted Intuitive Type (INFJ, INTJ):** Perception receives archetypal images arising from the depths, visions and symbolic impressions that converge toward a single meaning. This type sees what is coming, senses the underlying pattern, but struggles to translate inner certainty into terms the outer world can use. Sensation is inferior and manifests as clumsiness with the physical world, disconnection from the body, or sudden sensory overwhelm. The danger is possession by the vision: identification with the archetypal image to the point of prophetic inflation. Jung described this type as the most removed from external reality, a "mystic dreamer" whose insights, when communicated effectively, can be of extraordinary cultural significance, but who may otherwise remain misunderstood and isolated.

Each pair of MBTI types sharing a Jungian portrait is distinguished by its auxiliary (Good Parent) function: the ENTJ and ESTJ are both extraverted thinking types, but the ENTJ's Ni-auxiliary pulls toward strategic vision while the ESTJ's Si-auxiliary pulls toward established procedure. This auxiliary difference produces the distinct psychological texture of the portrait page, even though both share the same Jungian bedrock.
